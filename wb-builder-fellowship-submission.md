I saw a WindBorne billboard a few months ago and looked you up. Reading about what you do captured my imagination — I had never considered how a balloon can be steered by changing altitude to slip into a favorable stream, and found it particularly compelling that measurements taken by balloons are assimilated into forecasts that inform the decisions that determine their trajectories.

A few weeks ago, I read an engineering note, "Making WindBorne's Balloon Comms Self-Sufficient with Radio," and became curious about how such a system might work, so I decided to start building. Over the past ten days I built a balloon constellation simulator — a Rust sim server flying balloons on real ERA5 wind fields, a Cesium globe frontend, and a mesh where each balloon infers its own connectivity from the beacons it actually received. Getting the browser's view of the constellation to stay honest about how stale it was turned out to be similar to problems I'd worked on at Curia, where I built a system that streamed EEG, eye tracking, and other biometric sensors through LabStreamingLayer and synchronized them against game footage.

I had two additional reasons beyond curiosity. One was to modernize how I develop software: I wrote most of it with AI coding tools, on the advice of someone at WindBorne I spoke with a few months ago. The other was to have something I could actually show — most of the work I'd otherwise point to sits in private repos at places I've worked in the past.

I recently watched a talk from one of your engineers on treating agents as topology rather than tools — running them as @mentionable participants in Zulip, in the same topics where the work is already being discussed. My own use of these tools has been solitary by comparison: one person, one terminal. I'd like to see what they look like wired into a team.

I see there are some new ML roles, and though I'm tempted to apply, the Builder Fellowship is what I find most interesting right now — a chance to see the entire loop, from how the balloons and radiosondes are built to how their measurements feed the models that inform their navigation. That sounds like a fascinating six months.
